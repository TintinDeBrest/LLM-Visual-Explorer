# Conclusion — A few ideas to remember

## 1. Words — or “concepts” — have a geometry

They are not simply stored in a dictionary.

Models represent them in a **semantic space** with hundreds, sometimes thousands, of dimensions.

With **LLM Visual Explorer**, we can try to “see” this space — and begin to develop an **intuition** for how it is organized.

## 2. But we are looking through a keyhole

A space with hundreds of dimensions cannot be directly represented on a screen.

2D maps and 3D planetariums are **projections**: they preserve part of the structure, but necessarily distort or lose another part.

This is why visualization allows us to explore, while **similarities calculated in the original space** allow us to measure.

## 3. Semantic proximity is statistical, not logical

Two nearby concepts are not necessarily synonyms.

Their proximity reflects **statistical regularities learned by the model** during training.

A relationship may therefore match our human intuition… or sometimes surprise us.

And when a relationship surprises us, the map does not necessarily tell us **why**: above all, it shows us **where to ask the question**.

## 4. There is no single map of “meaning”

As we have seen by varying scenarios, languages and models, the resulting geometry can change — sometimes significantly.

The **model**, the **language**, the **words chosen**, as well as **tokenization** and **context**, all contribute to the representation we observe.

A map produced by a model is therefore not **the map of meaning**: it is one particular representation, produced under particular conditions.

## 5. Models learn our language…

…but not necessarily **our view of the world**.

From vast amounts of text, these systems learn statistical regularities from which **surprisingly complex structures and behaviors can emerge**.

Exploring them is therefore not simply about asking what the model “knows”, but about observing **how it organizes what we give it to represent**.
